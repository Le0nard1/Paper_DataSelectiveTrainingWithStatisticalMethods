\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The performance of a supervised model improves with the amount of data it is
trained on, but this improvement is far from uniform. Across many domains the
generalisation error follows an approximately power-law learning
curve: it falls quickly while data are scarce and then flattens into a long
regime of diminishing returns~\cite{hestness2017}. In this asymptotic tail each
additional example contributes less than the last, so that the late stages of
learning become the most troublesome part of the process, and reaching an
accuracy target that was cheap to approach can demand disproportionately large
amounts of further data~\cite{hestness2017,sorscher2022}. This plateau is not
simply a matter of possessing ``enough'' data in aggregate. A model can be
trained on a large dataset and still learn slowly, because the examples that
would most improve it are rare relative to the many that merely reconfirm what it
already represents.

Data and compute are, in practice, always limited resources, and the imbalance
is most acute in industrial settings. Whereas academic benchmarks often assume a
large, curated datapool, real deployments frequently operate under tight
constraints: labelled data may be expensive to annotate, protected for privacy
or regulatory reasons, or simply infeasible to obtain. Under such constraints the aggregate size of a
dataset is a poor proxy for its value, and the ability to extract the most
learning from each available example, rather than simply to acquire more
examples, becomes a crucial factor on model quality.

The machine-learning literature has long recognised that not every example is
equally useful, and the broad field of selective data sampling has repeatedly
shown that training on a well-chosen subset can match or exceed training on the
full dataset at a fraction of the cost~\cite{settles2009,sorscher2022,katharopoulos2018}.
Active learning, curriculum learning, hard-example mining and related methods all
exploit this observation in different ways. Their industrial applicability is,
however, narrower than their success on benchmarks suggests: each presupposes a
resource the industrial setting lacks, whether an source for fresh labels, a large
and redundant dataset to prune, or simply enough data for the model to judge
informativeness reliably. As a result, the strategies proven most effective in
principle map poorly onto the fixed-budget, fully-labelled conditions that
dominate industrial practice, as Section~\ref{sec:strategies} sets out in detail.

We therefore propose a data-selective training strategy built on statistical
weakspot identification. A weakspot is a compact region of the input space in
which a trained model performs poorly, often because that region was
under-represented in the data the model has seen so far~\cite{ovadia2019}. Our
previous work developed statistical methods that locate such regions in a trained
regression model from its error distribution alone, without access to ground
truth~\cite{weakspotprior}. The central idea of the present paper is to close the
loop: rather than treating weakspot identification only as a post-hoc diagnostic,
we use it to steer training, directing supervision preferentially to the regions
where the model currently knows the least. This is an intuitive principle, namely
to spend the next unit of data or compute where the model is weakest, and it fits
the fixed-dataset setting naturally, because it selects among data the model
already possesses rather than assuming an external source.

An intuitive principle is not, by itself, evidence. The efficacy of
weakspot-guided training must be demonstrated rather than assumed, and its
behaviour is unlikely to be uniform. Because a weakspot is defined relative to
the model's behaviour, the value of any selection depends on the state
of the model at the moment the selection is made. The method also introduces
additional parameters, among them how sharply to concentrate the selection on the
identified region, how much data to add, and how long to retrain, whose settings
may decide whether it helps or harms. A responsible
evaluation must therefore establish not only whether the approach works on
average but also how it depends on the training state and on these parameters,
and must consequently identify the conditions under which it is most, and least,
effective.

To this end we build a training pipeline on top of the weakspot-identification
methods of our previous work~\cite{weakspotprior} and compare it directly against
conventional training that draws data without regard to the model's weak regions.
We construct a controlled experimental setup in which the effect of
weakspot-guided selection can be isolated, measured against direct-training
baselines, and analysed across the model states and parameters identified above,
so that any advantage, or its absence, can be attributed to the selection
strategy rather than to incidental differences in the setup.

In summary, this paper makes three contributions. First, it reframes the
selection of informative data as the selection of data inside a model's
weakspots, a formulation suited to the fixed, fully-labelled datasets common in
industry. Second, it closes the loop from statistical weakspot identification to
training, giving a concrete weakspot-guided selection pipeline. Third, it
evaluates that pipeline against a random-selection baseline across a controlled
sweep, quantifying how its efficacy depends on the model state, the sharpness of
the selection, and the identification method.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:strategies} reviews the existing families of data-selective
training, clarifies how they relate and differ, explains why they are difficult
to apply to a fixed, fully-labelled dataset, and sets out the general challenges
any such method must confront. Section~\ref{sec:approach} presents our
weakspot-based selective-training method and the degrees of freedom it exposes,
and Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the experimental setup and the factors it
varies. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the experiments and analyses the
results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets their broader implications
and limitations, before the Conclusion summarises the study.

\section{Data-Selective Training Strategies}
\label{sec:strategies}

Data-selective training refers to any strategy that treats the choice of which
examples to learn from, and in what order or proportion, as a decision to be
optimised rather than left to uniform random sampling. The premise common to the
whole family is that examples differ in how much they can still teach a given
model, so that a fixed training budget spends better on some than on
others~\cite{katharopoulos2018,sorscher2022}. The families differ primarily in the
signal they use to score an example and in how that signal relates to the training
loop; we review the principal families below before drawing out the axes along
which they organise. We then turn, in Section~\ref{sec:fixed}, to their
limitations on a fixed, fully-labelled dataset and, in
Section~\ref{sec:challenges}, to the challenges they share.

\subsection{Families of Selection Strategies}
\label{sec:families}

Active learning scores unlabelled examples by expected informativeness and
requests labels for the most informative ones, iterating between querying and
retraining~\cite{settles2009,cohn1996}. Its canonical signal is model
uncertainty, favouring points near a decision boundary or with high predictive
variance, although diversity- and representativeness-based criteria such as
core-set selection instead choose points that best cover the input
distribution~\cite{sener2018}, and hybrid gradient-embedding methods combine the
two~\cite{ash2020}. Active learning is thus fundamentally an \emph{acquisition}
strategy: its purpose is to decide which of many unlabelled points are worth the
cost of a label.

Curriculum learning fixes the dataset and instead manipulates the order in which
examples are presented, beginning with easy examples and gradually introducing
harder ones, on the hypothesis that a well-shaped ordering speeds convergence and
improves the final optimum of a non-convex objective~\cite{bengio2009}.
Self-paced learning makes the same idea adaptive by letting the model, rather
than a fixed schedule, decide which examples are currently easy enough to
include, expanding the working set as training proceeds~\cite{kumar2010}. Both
re-use data the model already holds and both rank examples by difficulty, but
where curriculum learning relies on a predetermined difficulty measure,
self-paced learning derives it from the model's own loss and so couples the
ordering to the training state.

A third family moves in the opposite direction to curriculum learning,
concentrating training on the examples the model finds hardest. Online
hard-example mining selects, within each mini-batch, the examples of highest loss
and updates only on them, on the grounds that easy examples dominate the data yet
contribute little gradient~\cite{shrivastava2016}. Importance sampling formalises
the same intuition by drawing examples in proportion to a principled measure such
as the gradient norm, which provably reduces the variance of the stochastic
gradient and accelerates training~\cite{katharopoulos2018}. More recent work
refines ``hardest'' into ``most worth learning'', excluding examples that are
noisy or already learnt and retaining those that are learnable and not yet
learnt~\cite{mindermann2022}. These methods are \emph{reweighting} rather than
acquisition strategies, and their scoring signal is recomputed continually as the
model changes.

A fourth perspective treats selection as \emph{compression} of an existing
labelled dataset: given the full set, discard the examples that contribute least
so that training on the remainder is cheaper with little loss of accuracy. A good
pruning metric can, in principle, bend the power-law learning curve towards
exponential scaling, but only when the retained subset is chosen well and the
dataset is large enough to be redundant~\cite{sorscher2022}. Core-set methods
provide the same compression through a geometric coverage
guarantee~\cite{sener2018}.

Despite their varied vocabulary, these families organise along a few axes. The
first is the selection signal: model uncertainty (active learning), example
difficulty measured externally (curriculum) or internally (self-paced,
hard-example mining, importance sampling), or geometric coverage (core-set,
pruning). The second is temporal: whether examples are scored once, as in static
pruning or a fixed curriculum, or repeatedly against an evolving model, as in
self-paced, importance-sampling and prioritised-training schemes. The third, and
the most consequential for the present work, is whether the strategy acquires new
labelled data, as active learning does, or only rearranges and reweights data
already available, as all the others do. Our proposal shares its signal, namely
where the model is weak, with uncertainty-based active learning, but operates,
like curriculum and importance sampling, entirely within a dataset that is
already labelled.

\subsection{Limitations in Industry Settings}
\label{sec:fixed}

The setting that motivates this paper is the one that prevails in industry rather
than on benchmarks: a single, limited, fully-labelled dataset is fixed before
training begins. Judged against this
setting, several of the strategies above lose their footing. Active learning is
the clearest case, because its defining operation, querying an oracle for the
label of a chosen unlabelled point, presupposes both an unlabelled pool and an
affordable labelling channel, neither of which exists once the dataset is fixed
and fully labelled; its informativeness criteria remain conceptually valuable, but
its acquisition loop cannot run~\cite{settles2009,farquhar2021}. Data pruning and
core-set selection face the opposite problem: they are designed to \emph{remove}
data and pay off only when the dataset is large and
redundant~\cite{sorscher2022}, whereas an industrial dataset is typically small
enough that discarding data destroys information the model can ill afford to lose.
Curriculum and self-paced learning do apply, since they merely re-order fixed
data, but re-ordering alone creates no new information; on a small dataset the
model soon sees every example, and the schedule affects the optimisation path more
than the ceiling of what can ultimately be learnt~\cite{bengio2009,kumar2010}.
Hard-example mining and importance sampling likewise apply and can improve sample
efficiency, yet they score individual points and, on a small fixed dataset, tend
to re-emphasise the same handful of high-loss examples, some of which are
mislabelled or anomalous rather than genuinely
informative~\cite{shrivastava2016,mindermann2022}. What no existing family
provides, for the industrial case, is a strategy that neither assumes new labels
nor discards data, but instead decides, among the labelled data already held,
which examples to emphasise on the basis of where the model as a whole is failing
rather than which isolated points happen to have high loss. This is the gap the
weakspot-guided approach is designed to fill.

\subsection{Challenges of Data-Selective Training}
\label{sec:challenges}

Steering training towards a chosen subset of the data is not without risk, and
several failure modes recur across the whole family of methods; a strategy that
ignores them can perform worse than uniform sampling. We highlight the challenges
most relevant to a weakspot-guided approach.

The first is catastrophic failure through over-concentration. If selection
focuses too narrowly on one region of the input space, the model is effectively
trained on a shifting, non-stationary distribution, and neural networks are known
to overwrite previously acquired knowledge when their training distribution
changes, the phenomenon of catastrophic interference or catastrophic
forgetting~\cite{mccloskey1989,kirkpatrick2017}. A method that repeatedly pours
data into the current weakspot may therefore improve locally while degrading the
regions it has stopped rehearsing, so that the model never converges but instead
chases its own moving weak region. Preserving coverage of the whole input space
while still emphasising the weak region is thus a central tension.

The second challenge is that the value of a selection depends on the state of the
model that produced it. What is informative early in training, when the model is
globally inaccurate, differs from what is informative late in training, when only
isolated regions remain weak, and a signal computed against one model state can
be stale or misleading once the model has moved~\cite{mindermann2022}. Closely
related is the cold-start problem observed in active learning, where selection
based on an immature model performs worse than random sampling because the model
is not yet accurate enough to know where it is weak~\cite{chen2022coldstart}. Any
weakspot-guided scheme must therefore decide how often to re-identify the weak
region, and must expect its benefit to vary over the course of training.

A third challenge is statistical bias. By construction, selective training makes
the data the model learns from no longer representative of the deployment
distribution, and this shift can bias both the learned model and any risk
estimate computed from the selected data~\cite{farquhar2021}. Over-representing a
weak region teaches the model that the region is more common than it truly is,
which can distort its predictions elsewhere unless the selection is corrected or
the loss reweighted to account for the altered sampling
probabilities~\cite{farquhar2021,katharopoulos2018}.

A fourth challenge is the difficulty of distinguishing genuinely informative data
from merely difficult or corrupted data. Selection rules that chase high error
will, on real datasets, tend to select mislabelled, noisy or anomalous points,
because these produce large losses for reasons unrelated to useful
information~\cite{mindermann2022}. A weakspot located by elevated model error is
vulnerable to the same confound: a region may appear weak because it is genuinely
under-learnt or merely because its labels are noisy, and a robust method must
avoid over-investing in the latter.

Finally, when data are selected in batches rather than one point at a time, naive
scoring tends to choose many near-identical examples, since the most informative
points by an individual criterion are often close together; the resulting batch
is redundant and wastes budget that diversity-aware selection would spend
better~\cite{ash2020,chen2022coldstart}. A region-based weakspot criterion
partially mitigates this, because it targets an area rather than isolated points,
but it must still ensure that the emphasis it places within that area does not
collapse onto a redundant few examples.

Together these challenges frame the questions the rest of the paper investigates.
They motivate a method that emphasises a weak region without abandoning the rest
of the input space, that re-identifies the region as the model evolves, and whose
parameters can be tuned to trade focus against coverage; and they motivate an
experimental setup capable of detecting when weakspot-guided training helps, when
it is neutral, and when it does harm.

\section{Weakspot-Based Selective Data Training}
\label{sec:approach}

Every data-selective strategy surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:strategies} rests on a
single premise: within a dataset some examples teach a given model more than
others, and a fixed training budget is spent better on the informative examples
than on the redundant ones. The strategies differ only in how they score
informativeness, whether by predictive uncertainty, by example difficulty, by
gradient magnitude, or by geometric coverage. We adopt the same premise but change
the granularity at which informativeness is defined. Rather than scoring
individual points, we ask \emph{where} in the input space the model is least
competent and treat the data lying in that region as the most informative.
Selecting the most informative data then becomes selecting data inside the model's
weakspot, a reframing that suits the fixed, fully-labelled setting because it
chooses among data already held rather than querying an oracle for new labels.

Locating that region is itself a statistical estimation problem, and we solve it
with the weakspot-identification methods developed in our previous
work~\cite{weakspotprior}. The object these methods operate on is the model's
error distribution over the input space, obtained by evaluating the trained model
on a sample of inputs and recording its error at each. A weakspot is the compact
region in which this error concentrates, that is, the region occupied by the
\emph{extrema} of the error distribution rather than by its bulk. The
identification proceeds in two stages: a distribution-approximation method first
reconstructs a continuous error-intensity surface from the finite, non-uniform
sample of errors, and a parametric extractor then thresholds the upper extrema of
that surface to return a weakspot as a centroid and an elliptical extent. Because
the quantity of interest is the extremum of the error distribution, the small,
data-poor region of highest error, the approach draws naturally on estimators of
distributional extrema, from high-quantile regression to peaks-over-threshold
extreme-value statistics, alongside the smoother surface estimators that describe
the surrounding landscape~\cite{weakspotprior}.

Our contribution is to create the iterative loop from identifying a weakspot to
correcting it. The method runs as a sequence of eight distinct stages:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Setup} --- assemble the available dataset, optionally inducing a
    weakspot by withholding data from a chosen region.
  \item \textbf{Training} --- train an initial model on the assembled data.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation} --- evaluate the model on a sample of the input space
    and record its error at every point, yielding the error distribution.
  \item \textbf{Weakspot identification} --- locate the weakspot from the extrema
    of that error distribution.
  \item \textbf{Data selection} --- draw new labelled points in the neighbourhood
    of the identified weakspot.
  \item \textbf{Retraining} --- retrain the model on the selected data.
  \item \textbf{Re-evaluation} --- evaluate the retrained model on the same
    sample.
  \item \textbf{Overview} --- compare the retrained model against the model before
    selection and against the random baseline introduced below.
\end{enumerate}
In principle the evaluate--identify--select--retrain cycle (stages~3--6) may be
repeated as the model's weak region migrates; in this study we first analyse a single
round in order to isolate the effect of one targeted selection from the
confounding effects of iteration.

The stage that carries the method's essential freedom is the selection itself, and
we deliberately do not add every point that falls inside the identified region.
Instead, new points are drawn according to a \emph{selection distribution} centred
on the weakspot, a Gaussian kernel whose variance sets how tightly the added data
concentrate on the weak region. This variance is the central control of the
method, and it governs directly the tension set out in
Section~\ref{sec:challenges}. A small variance pours data almost entirely into the
weakspot; trained on so concentrated a sample, the model sharpens where it was
weak but risks the catastrophic forgetting of the regions no longer represented in
its training data~\cite{mccloskey1989,kirkpatrick2017}. A large variance spreads
the selection across the input space until, in the limit, it becomes
indistinguishable from uniform sampling and the advantage of targeting disappears.
Varying the variance therefore sweeps the method along the whole spectrum from
sharply focused to effectively unfocused selection, and identifying where on that
spectrum the guided selection is most effective is a central objective of the
study.

Several further degrees of freedom determine when the approach helps. The number
of points added fixes the budget spent on the weak region. The model state at
which the weakspot is identified, controlled by how long the initial model has
trained, determines whether the diagnosis is trustworthy or premature, an echo of
the cold-start problem in active learning, where a selection made against an
immature model can be worse than no targeting at all~\cite{chen2022coldstart}. The
retraining time governs how fully the model absorbs the new data. The choice of
detector determines how faithfully the extrema of the error distribution are
localised, and so how accurately the selection is aimed. Even whether a weakspot
is deliberately induced is a variation of interest, since the same procedure
applies unchanged to a model whose weakness is not manufactured by withholding
data but emerges only from label noise, distribution shift, function complexity,
and the finite size of the dataset. Each of these is a parameter whose influence on
efficacy the experiment is designed to quantify.

Because adding almost any data may improve a model, a demonstration that
weakspot-guided selection helps is meaningful only against a control that adds the
same amount of data without targeting. We therefore pair every guided selection
with a \emph{random baseline} that draws the same number of new points uniformly
at random, with no reference to the identified weakspot, and retrains under
identical conditions. This baseline stands in for unaltered training, the ordinary
practice of adding data without regard to where the model is weak, and it is the
reference against which the guided selection is judged. Comparing the two isolates
the contribution of the targeting from the contribution of merely enlarging the
training data, and it is this difference, rather than the improvement over the
initial model alone, that measures the value of the weakspot-based selection.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Task and Data}
We evaluate the method on a controlled synthetic regression task with known ground
truth, which is what makes an honest before-and-after comparison of a
strategy possible. The target is a sum of Gaussian bumps on the unit square;
labelled samples are drawn from it, and Gaussian noise is added to the training and
selected labels only, so that the evaluation error reflects genuine model failure
rather than label noise. Because the function is known analytically, so the model's 
true error ca be measured exactly and we have an external reference for accurate comparison. The regression
model is a multilayer perceptron of fixed architecture throughout.

\subsection{Pipeline Instantiation}
The experiment realises the eight stages of Section~\ref{sec:approach} directly, so
we state here only the concrete choices rather than repeating the procedure. The
\emph{setup} stage draws the dataset and, when a weakspot is required, induces it by
withholding every point within a chosen radius of a centre. \emph{Training} and
\emph{retraining} fit the multilayer perceptron for a set number of iterations, the
latter on the selected points alone, so that an over-concentrated selection is
penalised through the forgetting it induces. \emph{Evaluation} and
\emph{re-evaluation} score the model on a fresh uniform sample of the landscape,
which stands in for the deployment distribution. The \emph{weakspot-identification}
stage applies one of the detection methods, taken unchanged from our previous
work~\cite{weakspotprior}\todo{reference the previous weakspot-identification
paper}, and \emph{data selection} draws new points from a candidate pool through the
Gaussian selection kernel, with a matched uniform draw for the baseline. The
\emph{overview} stage compares the initial, guided, and baseline models.

\subsection{Swept Parameters}
To map the conditions under which the approach helps, we sweep the experiment over
the factors listed in Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, grouped by the stage to which each
belongs; their motivation was given in Section~\ref{sec:approach}. The gap radius
includes the value zero, which induces no weakspot at all, so that the method is
tested both on a deliberately created weak region and on weakness that is purely
emergent. Every configuration is run against all detection methods, so the detector
is itself a swept factor. The sweep is executed as a single resumable batch that
records one row per detector per configuration and appends results incrementally,
so the grid can be extended without recomputation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Model performance is measured by the \emph{mean absolute error} (MAE) of the
predictions on the held-out evaluation set against the noiseless ground truth. We
report the MAE of the initial model, of the guided-retrained model, and of the
random baseline, so that the value of the targeting is read directly as the
difference in MAE between the guided and baseline models. To distinguish a local
from a global effect, the same error is additionally averaged inside and outside
the induced weakspot whenever one is present. The accuracy of the weakspot
identification itself is assessed separately, with the two metrics of our previous
work~\cite{weakspotprior}: the centroid distance between the detected and the true
weakspot, and the intersection-over-union of their extents. These per-configuration
scores, each recorded for every detector, furnish the aggregate view analysed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Experiments and Analysis}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{A Broad Sweep: Weakspot Guidance Is Not Free}
\label{sec:broadsweep}
We first map the method's behaviour across the full factor grid of
Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, a total of $324{,}000$ configurations evaluated against each
of five detection methods, or slightly over $1.6$ million pipeline runs. The
headline result is a caution rather than a triumph. Guided selection improved on
the random baseline over the whole evaluation area in fewer than one configuration
in three ($31.7\%$), and the median configuration was in fact marginally worse than
random ($\Delta\mathrm{MAE}=+0.027$). Weakspot-guided training is therefore not a
universally safe substitute for uniform sampling: whether it helps or harms is
decided by how it is configured, and a careless instantiation spends its data
budget worse than adding points at random. This conditional character, rather than
any average effect, is the finding that governs the rest of the section.

Where guidance did win, the successful configurations were far from uniformly
distributed in the parameter space. Improvements concentrated on \emph{undertrained}
initial models, obtained with short initial training and small training sets, on
\emph{moderate-to-wide} selection kernels, and on \emph{low-to-moderate}
concentration of the added data, precisely the regime in which the model still has a
genuine, diagnosable weak region and the selection repairs it without starving the
rest of the input space. Fixing the single configuration that this analysis
identifies as robust, an induced gap of radius $0.12$, an initial model trained for
only $12$ iterations on $100$ points, a selection kernel of width $\sigma=0.5$ with a
rehearsal mix of $0.5$, and $100$ points added over $400$ retraining iterations,
reduced the whole-area MAE from $0.95$ for the initial model to $0.48$, against
$0.57$ for the matched random baseline. Guidance thus recovered roughly half of the
initial error and beat the random baseline by $0.094$ in MAE (about $16\%$), winning
in $64\%$ of the individual seed--detector runs; inside the induced weakspot the
effect was larger still, the mean error falling from $3.12$ to $1.25$ against $1.68$
for the baseline.

\subsection{Which Parameters Decide the Outcome}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
The swept factors do not matter equally. Fig.~\ref{fig:effect} isolates each
parameter around the operating point and measures the span of the whole-area
advantage $\Delta = \mathrm{MAE}_\mathrm{random}-\mathrm{MAE}_\mathrm{guided}$ that
it induces when varied alone. Four dimensions dominate. The \emph{selection-kernel
width} is the single most decisive, swinging the advantage by $0.25$ in MAE across
its range and, on its own, turning a clear win into a clear loss; the \emph{number
of curated points added}, the \emph{initial training time} that sets the model state
at which the weakspot is diagnosed, and the \emph{induced gap size} each move it by
roughly $0.18$ to $0.20$. These are the consequential dimensions of the method, and
they map directly onto the challenges of Section~\ref{sec:challenges}: the model
state governs whether the diagnosis is trustworthy, the cold-start concern, while
the kernel width and the amount of data added govern the focus--coverage trade-off.
The rehearsal mix shows a smaller isolated span at this wide-kernel operating point,
but that understates its role; as the next subsection shows, it becomes decisive
precisely when the kernel is narrow, because it is then the only remaining source of
coverage.

\subsection{Focus versus Coverage: The Rehearsal Mix}
\label{sec:mix}
The tension identified in Section~\ref{sec:challenges}, between concentrating data on
the weak region and preserving coverage of the rest of the input space, is governed
by two interchangeable controls, and Fig.~\ref{fig:isolation} isolates each in turn
over $50$ random seeds. The left panel fixes a deliberately narrow selection kernel
($\sigma=0.1$), so that the rehearsal mix $\alpha$ is the sole route to coverage, and
sweeps it from pure uniform rehearsal ($\alpha=0$) to pure weakspot focus
($\alpha=1$). The resulting curve traces the paper's argument in a single sweep. At
$\alpha=0$ the guided selection is by construction identical in distribution to the
random baseline, and the two coincide. As a little guidance is introduced,
$\alpha\in[0.05,0.35]$, the guided model pulls below the baseline and wins: a modest
bias towards the weak region helps. Past a crossover near $\alpha\approx0.4$ the
balance inverts, and as $\alpha\to1$ the model, now trained almost entirely inside
the pinpoint weakspot, forgets the rest of the space catastrophically, its
whole-area error climbing above even that of the untrained initial model. The
progression from parity, through advantage, to catastrophic forgetting is exactly
the behaviour the challenge anticipates, and it makes explicit why the method must be
prevented from over-concentrating.

The right panel shows that the kernel width is a second, independent route to the
same effect: holding the selection purely guided ($\alpha=1$) and instead widening
the kernel reproduces the identical arc in mirror image, a narrow kernel forgetting
just as a high mix does, and a wide kernel converging back to the baseline just as
$\alpha\to0$ does. The two mechanisms are thus substitutable, coverage being
restored either by mixing in uniform rehearsal or by widening the kernel, and pure
focus, reached by either route, forgets. This equivalence explains why the broad
sweep of Section~\ref{sec:broadsweep} rewarded low mix \emph{or} wide kernels
interchangeably, and it delimits the safe operating region for the method as any
combination that keeps a non-trivial fraction of the added data spread across the
input space.
