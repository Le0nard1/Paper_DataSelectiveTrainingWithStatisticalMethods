\begin{abstract}
Supervised learning reaches a regime of diminishing returns long before a dataset is exhausted, and in industrial practice that dataset is fixed, fully labelled and small. The only remaining lever is to extract more from the data already held. Established data-selective training strategies each rest on assumptions ill-suited to this setting,
whether an additional labelling source, an external ordering of example difficulty, or a dataset large enough to prune, yet all share one idea: a training budget is spent better on a curated set of the most informative data points.
We adopt that idea from a different
perspective, reframing informative-data selection as selection inside a model's
weakspots, the compact regions in which its error concentrates, and closing the loop from statistical weakspot identification to training. 
To proof and analyse the approach we
designed a controlled regression setup with known ground truth, matching every guided data
selection against a random-selection baseline. Its effective implementation depends
on several factors, among them the sharpness of the selection, the state of the model
when the weakspot is identified, and the identification method itself. Guided
selection in our settings improved on the baseline in only 31.7\% of the configurations examined, so
it demands careful and sensitive application rather than adoption by default. The
most significant factor proved to be the mix of curated with uniformly drawn data,
the counter to one of the central challenges of data-centric training, catastrophic
forgetting. Set well, it reduces whole-area error by $16\%$ against the baseline and the
model's error inside the weak region from $3.12$ to $1.25$, allowing us to optimise
training successfully within the stated restrictions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The performance of a supervised model improves with the amount of data it is
trained on, but this improvement is far from uniform. Across many domains the
generalisation error follows an approximately power-law learning
curve: it falls quickly while data are scarce and then flattens into a long
regime of diminishing returns~\cite{hestness2017}. In this asymptotic tail each
additional example contributes less than the last, so that the late stages of
learning become the most troublesome part of the process, and reaching an
accuracy target that was cheap to approach can demand disproportionately large
amounts of further data~\cite{hestness2017,sorscher2022}. This plateau is not
simply a matter of possessing ``enough'' data in aggregate. A model can be
trained on a large dataset and still learn slowly, because the examples that
would most improve it are rare relative to the many that merely reconfirm what it
already represents.

Data and compute are, in practice, always limited, and the imbalance is most acute in
industrial settings, where labelled data may be expensive to annotate, protected for
privacy or regulatory reasons, or simply infeasible to obtain. Under such constraints
the aggregate size of a dataset is a poor proxy for its value, and the ability to
extract the most learning from each available example, rather than to acquire more,
becomes decisive for model quality. The machine-learning literature has long recognised
that not every example is equally useful, and selective data sampling, through active
learning, curriculum learning, hard-example mining and related methods, has repeatedly
shown that a well-chosen subset can match or exceed the full dataset at a fraction of
the cost~\cite{settles2009,sorscher2022,katharopoulos2018}. Their industrial
applicability is, however, narrower than their benchmark success suggests: each
presupposes a resource the industrial setting lacks, whether a source of fresh labels, a
large and redundant dataset to prune, or simply enough data to judge informativeness
reliably, so that the strategies proven most effective in principle map poorly onto the
fixed-budget, fully-labelled conditions that dominate industrial practice, as
Section~\ref{sec:strategies} sets out in detail.

We therefore propose a data-selective training strategy built on statistical
weakspot identification. A weakspot is a compact region of the input space in
which a trained model performs poorly, often because that region was
under-represented in the data seen so far~\cite{ovadia2019}. Our
previous work developed statistical methods that locate such regions in a trained
regression model from its error distribution alone, without access to ground
truth~\cite{weakspotprior}. \todo{reference the
specific detector comparison in the companion paper}
The present paper closes the
loop: rather than treating weakspot identification only as a post-hoc diagnostic,
we use it to steer training, spending the next unit of data where the model is weakest.
This fits the fixed-dataset setting naturally, because it selects among data the application
already possesses rather than assuming an external source.

An intuitive principle is not, by itself, evidence, and the behaviour of
weakspot-guided training is unlikely to be uniform. Because a weakspot is defined
relative to the model's behaviour, the value of any selection depends on the state of
the model when the selection is made; the method also introduces further parameters,
among them how sharply to concentrate the selection, how much data to add, and how long
to retrain, whose settings may decide whether it helps or harms. A responsible
evaluation must therefore establish not only whether the approach works on average but
how it depends on the training state and on these parameters, and identify the
conditions under which it is most, and least, effective. To this end we build a training
pipeline on the weakspot-identification methods of our previous work~\cite{weakspotprior}
and compare it, in a controlled setup that isolates the effect of guided selection,
against conventional training that draws data randomly.

In summary, this paper makes three contributions. First, it reframes the
selection of informative data as the selection of data inside a model's
weakspots. Second, it closes the loop from statistical weakspot identification to
training, giving a concrete weakspot-guided selection pipeline. Third, it
evaluates that pipeline against a random-selection baseline across a controlled
sweep, quantifying how its efficacy depends on the model state, the sharpness of
the selection, and the identification method.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:strategies} reviews the existing families of data-selective
training, clarifies how they relate and differ, explains why they are difficult
to apply to a fixed, fully-labelled dataset, and sets out the general challenges
any such method must confront. Section~\ref{sec:approach} presents our
weakspot-based selective-training method and the degrees of freedom it exposes,
and Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the experimental setup and the factors under
observation. The experiments then proceed in three stages: Section~\ref{sec:broadsweep}
maps with a broad sweep where weakspot guidance helps and where it harms and fixes the
operating point, after which Section~\ref{sec:mix} isolates the coverage balance that
governs catastrophic forgetting and Section~\ref{sec:detector} the choice of
identification method. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the broader implications
and limitations, before the Conclusion summarises the study.

\section{Data-Selective Training Strategies}
\label{sec:strategies}

Data-selective training treats the choice of which examples to learn from, and in
what order or proportion, as a decision to be optimised rather than left to uniform
random sampling. Its premise is that examples differ in how much they can still
teach a given model, so that a fixed training budget spends better on some than on
others~\cite{katharopoulos2018,sorscher2022}. The families below differ in the
signal they use to score an example and in how that signal enters the training loop.

\subsection{Families of Selection Strategies}
\label{sec:families}

Active learning scores unlabelled examples by expected informativeness and
requests labels for the most informative ones, iterating between querying and
retraining~\cite{settles2009,cohn1996}. Its canonical signal is model
uncertainty, favouring points near a decision boundary or with high predictive
variance. Diversity-based criteria such as core-set selection instead choose
points that best cover the input distribution~\cite{sener2018}, and hybrid
gradient-embedding methods combine the two~\cite{ash2020}. Active learning is
thus an \emph{acquisition} strategy: it decides which of many unlabelled points
are worth the cost of a label.

Curriculum learning fixes the dataset and manipulates the order of presentation,
beginning with easy examples and introducing harder ones gradually, on the
hypothesis that a well-shaped ordering speeds convergence and improves the final
optimum of a non-convex objective~\cite{bengio2009}. Self-paced learning makes
the idea adaptive, letting the model rather than a fixed schedule decide which
examples are currently easy enough to include~\cite{kumar2010}. Both re-use data
already held and rank it by difficulty; they differ in that curriculum learning
relies on a predetermined difficulty measure whereas self-paced learning derives
one from the model's own loss, coupling the ordering to the training state.

A third family moves in the opposite direction, concentrating training on the
examples the model finds hardest. Online hard-example mining updates only on the
highest-loss examples of each mini-batch, on the grounds that easy examples
dominate the data yet contribute little gradient~\cite{shrivastava2016}.
Importance sampling formalises the same intuition by drawing examples in
proportion to a principled measure such as the gradient norm, provably reducing
the variance of the stochastic gradient~\cite{katharopoulos2018}. More recent
work refines ``hardest'' into ``most worth learning'', excluding examples that
are noisy or already learnt~\cite{mindermann2022}. These are \emph{reweighting}
rather than acquisition strategies, and their scoring signal is recomputed as the
model changes.

A fourth perspective treats selection as \emph{compression}: given the full
labelled set, discard the examples that contribute least so that training on the
remainder is cheaper with little loss of accuracy. A good pruning metric can bend
the power-law learning curve towards exponential scaling, but only when the
retained subset is chosen well and the dataset is large enough to be
redundant~\cite{sorscher2022}. Core-set methods achieve the same compression
through a geometric coverage guarantee~\cite{sener2018}.

These families differ in their scoring signal and in whether an example is scored
once or repeatedly against an evolving model, but the distinction that matters
here is simpler: active learning acquires new labelled data, whereas every other
family only rearranges and reweights data already available.

\subsection{Limitations in Industry Settings}
\label{sec:fixed}

The setting that motivates this paper prevails in industry rather than on benchmarks:
a single, limited, fully-labelled dataset is fixed before training begins, and the
practitioner has only partial control over how it was acquired. Judged against it,
several of the strategies above lose their footing. Active learning is the clearest
case, since additional querying presupposes both an unlabelled pool and an affordable labelling channel, and neither exists once the dataset is fixed and fully labelled.
Because acquisition is moreover only partly under the practitioner's control, the
examples its criterion asks for often cannot be obtained even in principle; its
informativeness criteria remain conceptually valuable, but its acquisition loop cannot
run~\cite{settles2009,farquhar2021}.

The remaining families apply in principle yet deliver little. Curriculum and self-paced
learning merely re-order fixed data, but an industrial problem is rarely understood well
enough to rank examples by difficulty, and re-ordering creates no new information: on a
small dataset the model soon sees every example, so the schedule shapes the optimisation
path rather than the ceiling of what can be learnt~\cite{bengio2009,kumar2010}. Data
pruning and core-set selection point the wrong way, being designed to \emph{remove} data
and paying off only on large, redundant datasets~\cite{sorscher2022}, whereas an
industrial dataset is typically small enough that discarding data destroys information
the model can ill afford to lose. Hard-example mining and importance sampling score
individual points and, on a small fixed dataset, re-emphasise the same handful of
high-loss examples, some mislabelled or anomalous rather than genuinely
informative~\cite{shrivastava2016,mindermann2022}.

The weakspot-guided approach developed in
Section~\ref{sec:approach} is designed to fill this gap.

\subsection{Challenges of Data-Selective Training}
\label{sec:challenges}

Steering training towards a chosen subset is not without risk, and a strategy that
ignores the recurring failure modes can perform worse than uniform sampling. We
highlight the three most relevant to a weakspot-guided approach.

The first is catastrophic forgetting through over-concentration. Focusing selection too
narrowly trains the model on a shifting, non-stationary distribution, and neural networks
overwrite previously acquired knowledge when that distribution
changes~\cite{mccloskey1989,kirkpatrick2017}. A method that keeps pouring data into the
current weakspot therefore improves locally while degrading the regions it no longer
rehearses. Over-representing a weak region also shifts the training distribution away
from deployment, biasing predictions elsewhere unless the sampling is corrected or the
loss reweighted~\cite{farquhar2021,katharopoulos2018}. Preserving coverage of the whole
input space while still emphasising the weak region is thus the central tension.

The second is that a selection is only as good as the model that produced it. What is
informative early in training, when the model is globally inaccurate, differs from what
is informative late, and a signal computed against an immature model can be stale or
misleading. This is the cold-start problem familiar from active learning, where selection
on an under-trained model performs worse than
random~\cite{mindermann2022,chen2022coldstart}. A weakspot-guided scheme must therefore
expect its benefit to vary with the training state.

The third is that high error need not mean high information. Selection rules that chase
error tend to select mislabelled, noisy or anomalous points, whose losses are large for
reasons unrelated to useful learning~\cite{mindermann2022}. A weakspot located by
elevated error faces the same confound: a region may be genuinely under-learnt or merely
noisy, and a robust method must not over-invest in the latter.

Together these motivate a method that emphasises a weak region without abandoning the
rest of the input space, re-identifies it as the model evolves, and exposes parameters
that trade focus against coverage, and an experimental setup able to tell when guided
training helps, when it is neutral, and when it harms.

\section{Weakspot-Based Selective Data Training}
\label{sec:approach}

We adopt the premise common to every strategy of Section~\ref{sec:strategies} but
change the granularity at which informativeness is defined. Rather than scoring
individual points, we ask \emph{where} in the input space the model is least
competent and treat the data lying in that region as the most informative.
Selecting the most informative data then becomes selecting data inside the model's
weakspot, a reframing that suits the fixed, fully-labelled setting because it
chooses among data already held rather than querying an oracle for new labels.

Locating that region is itself a statistical estimation problem, and we solve it
with the weakspot-identification methods developed in our previous
work~\cite{weakspotprior}. The object these methods operate on is the model's
error distribution over the input space, obtained by evaluating the trained model
on a sample of inputs and recording its error at each. A weakspot is the compact
region in which this error concentrates, that is, the region occupied by the
\emph{extrema} of the error distribution rather than by its bulk.

Our contribution is to create the iterative loop from identifying a weakspot to
correcting it. The method runs as a sequence of eight distinct stages:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Setup} --- assemble the available dataset, optionally inducing a
    weakspot by withholding data from a chosen region.
  \item \textbf{Training} --- train an initial model on the assembled data.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation} --- evaluate the model on a sample of the input space
    and record its error at every point, yielding the error distribution.
  \item \textbf{Weakspot identification} --- locate the weakspot from the extrema
    of that error distribution.
  \item \textbf{Data selection} --- draw new labelled points in the neighbourhood
    of the identified weakspot.
  \item \textbf{Retraining} --- retrain the model on the selected data.
  \item \textbf{Re-evaluation} --- evaluate the retrained model on the same
    sample.
  \item \textbf{Overview} --- compare the retrained model against the model before
    selection and against the random baseline introduced below.
\end{enumerate}
In principle the evaluate--identify--select--retrain cycle (stages~3--6) may be
repeated as the model's weak region migrates; in this study we first analyse a single
round in order to isolate the effect of one targeted selection from the
confounding effects of iteration.

The stage that carries the method's essential freedom is the selection itself, and
we deliberately do not add every point that falls inside the identified region.
Instead, new points are drawn according to a \emph{selection distribution} centred
on the weakspot, a Gaussian kernel whose variance sets how tightly the added data
concentrate on the weak region. This variance is the central control of the
method, and it governs directly the tension set out in
Section~\ref{sec:challenges}. A small variance pours data almost entirely into the
weakspot; trained on so concentrated a sample, the model sharpens where it was
weak but risks the catastrophic forgetting of the regions no longer represented in
its training data~\cite{mccloskey1989,kirkpatrick2017}

Several further degrees of freedom determine the efficacy. The number
of points added fixes the budget spent on the weak region. The model state at
which the weakspot is identified, controlled by how long the initial model has
trained, determines whether the diagnosis is trustworthy or premature, an echo of
the cold-start problem in active learning, where a selection made against an
immature model can be worse than no targeting at all~\cite{chen2022coldstart}. The
retraining time governs how fully the model absorbs the new data. The choice of
detector determines how faithfully the extrema of the error distribution are
localised, and so how accurately the selection is aimed. Even whether a weakspot
is deliberately induced is a variation of interest, since the same procedure
applies unchanged to a model whose weakness is not manufactured by withholding
data but emerges only from label noise, distribution shift, function complexity,
and the finite size of the dataset. Each of these is a parameter whose influence on
efficacy the experiment is designed to quantify.

Because adding almost any data may improve a model, a demonstration that
weakspot-guided selection helps is meaningful only against a control that adds the
same amount of data without guidance. We therefore pair every guided selection
with a \emph{random baseline} that draws the same number of new points uniformly
at random, with no reference to the identified weakspot, and retrains under
identical conditions. This baseline stands in for unaltered training and it is the
reference against which the guided selection is judged. Comparing the two isolates
the contribution of the guided from the contribution of merely enlarging the
training data, and it is this difference, rather than the improvement over the
initial model alone, that measures the value of the weakspot-based selection.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Task and Data}
We evaluate the method on a controlled synthetic regression task with known ground
truth, which is what makes an honest before-and-after comparison of a
strategy possible. The target is a sum of Gaussian bumps on the unit square;
labelled samples are drawn from it, and Gaussian noise is added to the training and
selected labels only, so that the evaluation error reflects genuine model failure
rather than label noise. Because the function is known analytically, the model's true error can be measured
exactly, giving an external reference for an accurate before-and-after comparison. The
regression model is a multilayer perceptron of fixed architecture throughout.

\subsection{Pipeline Instantiation}
The experiment realises the eight stages of Section~\ref{sec:approach} directly; we
state only the concrete choices. A weakspot, where required, is induced by withholding
every point within a chosen radius of a centre. Training and retraining fit the
multilayer perceptron for a set number of iterations, the latter on the selected points
alone, so that an over-concentrated selection is penalised through the forgetting it
induces. Both evaluations score the model on a fresh uniform sample of the landscape,
which stands in for the deployment distribution. Identification applies one of the
detection methods, taken unchanged from our previous
work~\cite{weakspotprior}\todo{reference the previous weakspot-identification paper},
and selection draws new points from a candidate pool through a variable Gaussian selection kernel and distribution mix ratio.

\subsection{Swept Parameters}
To map the conditions under which the approach helps, we sweep the experiment over
the factors listed in Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, grouped by the stage to which each
belongs. The gap radius
includes the value zero, which induces no weakspot at all, so that the method is
tested both on a deliberately created weak region and on weakness that is purely
emergent. The sweep is executed as a single resumable batch that
records one row per detector per configuration and appends results incrementally,
so the grid can be extended without recomputation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Model performance is measured by the mean absolute error (MAE) of the
predictions on the held-out evaluation set against the noiseless ground truth. We
report the MAE of the initial model, of the guided-retrained model, and of the
random baseline, so that the value of the targeting is read directly as the
difference in MAE between the guided and baseline models. To distinguish a local
from a global effect, the same error is additionally averaged inside and outside
the induced weakspot whenever one is present. The accuracy of the weakspot
identification itself is assessed separately, with the two metrics of our previous
work~\cite{weakspotprior}: the centroid distance between the detected and the true
weakspot, and the intersection-over-union of their extents. These per-configuration
scores, each recorded for every detector, furnish the aggregate view analysed in
Section~\ref{sec:broadsweep}.

\section{The Dataset Mix: Countering Catastrophic Forgetting}
\label{sec:mix}
The broad sweep singles out catastrophic forgetting (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}) as
the dominant risk, and the two coverage controls, the rehearsal mix and the kernel
width, were introduced to counter it. Fig.~\ref{fig:isolation} isolates each over $50$
seeds. The left panel fixes a narrow kernel ($\sigma=0.1$) and sweeps the rehearsal mix
$\alpha$ from pure uniform rehearsal ($\alpha=0$) to pure guided focus ($\alpha=1$). At
$\alpha=0$ guided selection coincides with the random baseline by construction; for a
modest bias ($\alpha\in[0.05,0.35]$) it pulls below the baseline and wins; and past a
crossover near $\alpha\approx0.4$ the model, trained almost entirely inside the pinpoint
weakspot, forgets the rest of the space catastrophically, its whole-area error rising
above even that of the untrained initial model. This progression from parity through
advantage to catastrophic forgetting is exactly what the challenge anticipates.

The right panel shows the kernel width to be a second, substitutable route to the same
effect: holding the selection purely guided ($\alpha=1$) and widening the kernel
reproduces the arc in mirror image, a narrow kernel forgetting as a high mix does and a
wide kernel converging back to the baseline. Coverage can thus be restored either by
mixing in uniform rehearsal or by widening the kernel, which is why the broad sweep
rewarded low mix \emph{or} wide kernels interchangeably; the safe operating region is
any combination that keeps a non-trivial fraction of the added data spread across the
input space.

\section{The Identification Method}
\label{sec:detector}
Because the sweep's dimensionality made evaluating every detector prohibitive, the
broad sweep held the identification method to five detectors chosen for their strong and
diverse performance in our previous work~\cite{weakspotprior}\todo{reference the
specific detector comparison in the companion paper}: EVT\,$\times$\,GPR (geometric),
kNN performance mapping, peaks-over-threshold (EVT), GPR/kNN (variance-weighted) and RBF
interpolation. This experiment lifts that restriction, fixing every factor at the
operating point and isolating the identification method across all seventeen detectors
of the companion work over twenty seeds, each sharing the same initial model, induced
gap and candidate pool. Two findings emerge from Fig.~\ref{fig:detector}. First, in this
favourable regime the method is robust to the choice of detector: every one of the
seventeen beats the random baseline, over the whole area and inside the weakspot.
Second, the improvement varies almost tenfold, from $0.157$ in whole-area MAE for the
strongest, quantile regression, to $0.016$ for the weakest, which are exactly those that
fail to localise the gap (centroid distance near $0.5$, intersection-over-union near
zero) and retain an advantage only through the uniform rehearsal they still contain.
This is the third challenge of Section~\ref{sec:challenges} in operation, that a
selection rule is only as trustworthy as the region it identifies. That quantile
regression attains the largest benefit with only a moderate overlap score further
suggests that placing the selection mass in the right place matters more than capturing
the region's exact extent.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central result of this study is a conditional one. Weakspot-guided selection is not
a free improvement over adding data at random: across the full grid it beat the matched
baseline in fewer than one configuration in three, and the median configuration was
marginally worse. At a well-chosen operating point, however, the same method cut the
whole-area error by $16\%$ relative to that baseline, and the error inside the weak
region to roughly two-fifths of its value before retraining. The distance between these
two readings is itself the finding. Guidance is an instrument whose sign is decided by
how it is configured. Reporting only its favourable configuration, the ordinary
temptation when a method is proposed, would have described a different and far more
flattering method.

What decides that sign is coverage rather than aim. The sensitivity ordering places the
rehearsal mix ratio and the selection-kernel width ahead of every other factor, well
ahead of the state of the initial model and far ahead of the descriptors of the task.
These two are the controls of the balance between concentrating data on the weak region
and preserving the rest of the input space. The isolation experiments explain their
dominance. Sweeping the rehearsal mix under a narrow kernel produces a single monotone
progression: parity with the baseline, then a genuine advantage at modest bias, then a
collapse past a crossover near $\alpha\approx0.4$ at which the whole-area error exceeds
even that of the model before retraining. Sweeping the kernel width under pure guidance
reproduces the arc in mirror image. The operative constraint is therefore not how
accurately the weak region is located but how much of the remaining input space survives
the retraining, and the first challenge of Section~\ref{sec:challenges} is the binding
one rather than one risk among several. That the two controls are substitutable is a
practical convenience, but it also means they must be set jointly: a narrow kernel is
safe only with ample rehearsal, and vice versa.

The dependence on the initial model runs against the expectation drawn from active
learning. The best configurations were those in which selection was performed against a
deliberately undertrained model, whereas the cold-start literature holds that a
selection made on an immature model is unreliable and can be worse than
random~\cite{chen2022coldstart}. Two opposing forces are at work: the reliability of the
diagnosis improves as the model matures, while the headroom available to any
intervention shrinks. Our induced deficit is a coarse, spatially large gap that even a
crudely fitted model exposes clearly, so the reliability term is weak and headroom
dominates. A near-converged model has little left to gain, and redistributing its
training distribution is then more likely to disturb than to improve it. The
intervention therefore belongs early, while the error surface is still strongly
structured, rather than as a final polishing step. Where the deficit is subtle rather
than coarse the balance may reverse, so this is a boundary of our conclusion rather than
a general law.

The identification method proved the least critical of the three axes, but the shape of
its influence is instructive. At the operating point every one of the seventeen
detectors beat the random baseline, so the approach does not depend on a fortunate
choice of detector. The margins nonetheless differ almost tenfold, and the weakest are
precisely those that fail to localise the gap at all. That the strongest detector,
quantile regression, attains its advantage with only a moderate
intersection-over-union suggests that placing the selection mass approximately right
matters more than recovering the region's exact extent, which is encouraging because
approximate placement is the cheaper half of the problem. The failing detectors carry a
methodological warning. A guided pipeline configured for safety contains a substantial
uniform rehearsal component, so it can post a positive result even when its targeting
conveys no information. An apparently successful guided run is therefore not by itself
evidence that the identification worked, and any future evaluation of such a pipeline
should retain a matched control.

The factors describing the task, by contrast, were nearly inert. This cuts both ways.
The efficacy of the method appears to be governed by its own configuration rather than
by the problem, which allows an operating point tuned on one task to serve as a
plausible starting point on another; but it also denies the practitioner any cheap
screening rule, since no dataset property predicts whether guidance will pay. Notably,
the average advantage changed little when no weakspot was induced at all, so the
behaviour we report is not an artefact of a manufactured deficit.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The evaluation is deliberately synthetic
and two-dimensional, which is what makes the error measurable against a noiseless
reference and the weakspot's true extent known. Low dimension also makes uniform
rehearsal far easier than it would be on industrial data, where covering the input space
by uniform sampling is unachievable at any realistic budget; the coverage mechanism that
dominates our results would there have to be approximated. Retraining used the selected
points alone, a conservative protocol that makes the coverage question visible by
penalising over-concentration. Fine-tuning on the union of old and new data, as
industrial practice would ordinarily do, should shift the crossover and soften the
collapse, so the threshold near $\alpha\approx0.4$ is specific to this protocol rather
than a property of the method. We analysed a single round of the cycle, leaving the
repeated loop untested. Finally, the operating point was read off the same sweep that
reports its advantage, so the $16\%$ figure is optimistic as a point estimate; the
qualitative claims rest on the isolation experiments over fresh seeds. One model class
was used throughout.

These limitations map onto the work that follows. Iterating the loop with a schedule
that relaxes focus as the weak region shrinks is the obvious extension, expressible
through either coverage control. Moving to higher-dimensional and genuinely industrial
data would test the mechanism where rehearsal must be approximated rather than sampled.
The third challenge of Section~\ref{sec:challenges}, that high error need not indicate
high information, was not put under real strain here, because the label noise we swept
was uniform across the input space and so never produced a region that is irreducibly
noisy rather than under-learnt; heteroscedastic noise would create that confound.
Correcting the distributional bias the selection introduces, by importance weighting or
a reweighted loss~\cite{farquhar2021,katharopoulos2018}, is a further avenue.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper reframed the selection of informative training data as the selection of data
inside a model's weakspots, a formulation suited to the fixed, fully-labelled and
limited datasets that prevail in industry and that defeat the acquisition- and
pruning-based strategies proven on benchmarks. We closed the loop from statistical
weakspot identification to training with a concrete eight-stage pipeline whose selection
kernel and rehearsal mix trade focus against coverage, and evaluated it against a matched
random-selection baseline over $324{,}000$ configurations and more than $1.6$ million
pipeline runs on a controlled regression task with analytically known ground truth.

The evaluation shows that weakspot guidance is a conditional instrument rather than a
free improvement. It beat the baseline in only $31.7\%$ of configurations and its median
configuration was marginally worse, yet at a favourable operating point it reduced the
whole-area error by $16\%$ against that baseline and the in-weakspot error of the
initial model from $3.12$ to $1.25$. Which outcome obtains is determined chiefly by the balance between
concentrating data on the weak region and preserving coverage of the rest, controlled
interchangeably by the rehearsal mix or the kernel width, and much less by the task or by
the identification method, since at a sound operating point all seventeen detectors of
our previous work beat random selection. The benefit is largest when the model is still
undertrained and headroom remains. Weakspot-guided training is therefore best understood
as a redistribution of a fixed data budget whose gain is bounded by the coverage it
sacrifices; applied within that constraint, and judged against a control that isolates
the targeting from the mere addition of data, it delivers a substantial improvement, and
a considerably larger one where the model was weakest.
